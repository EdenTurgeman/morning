## 1. The frame

**Mechanism, one sentence.** The load is deliberately fixed for a whole session,
so reps are the only progress signal that exists — and the app puts last time's
number for *this exact set* in front of you while you do that set, so that the
moment you pass it is an event.

**The real scene.** 6:10am. Standing on the floor of his own home, half awake,
20 minutes before a shower. The phone is on the floor or a shelf a metre or two
away, sometimes seen upside down over a push-up. Sweaty hands, one knuckle,
offline, alone. Nobody else will ever see this screen.

**Cultural home.** A 30-year-old sommelier who trains at dawn. His daily
graphic traditions are not fitness ones: cellar books and tasting sheets,
vintage charts, appellation maps, label typography, printed reference tables.
The training half of his world is logbooks and plate arithmetic. The dawn half
is almanacs and twilight tables.

**What the first surface must prove.** That you can tell, from a metre and a
half away with one eye open, what you are doing, what you did last time, and
the instant you pass it — without the screen ever scrolling and without the
number being the only thing carrying the load.

### The rut, kept out of the list

- **What this category always ships:** a near-black screen, one enormous number,
  a coloured progress ring, a gradient. *This is also what the app currently is,
  and what Eden rejected on 2026-08-27.*
- **Its predictable opposite:** a bright flat white Swiss-grid app, one enormous
  number, hairline rules, tracked mono labels.
- **The brief's own picture:** the brief hands me a governing metaphor — dawn,
  sunrise, the sky. Its literal reading gets **at most one candidate** (#2, and
  it takes the *printed* form of dawn rather than the picture of one). The
  other six are derived from elsewhere in his world.

## 2. Seven grounded candidates, ordered by resonance

Ranked by my own honest judgement of how well each carries the mechanism for
this audience. Five material families are represented; no family holds more
than three.

| # | System | Family | Why it carries the mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The cellar book / tasting sheet** | paper, ink, rule | The artifact for "record what you did and set it against last time" already exists in his professional life, and it is ruled paper with a column of marks. Reps-against-last-time *is* a comparative tasting. |
| 2 | **The nautical almanac / twilight table** | printed technical reference | The session genuinely runs through astronomical → nautical → civil twilight. This is dawn in its *printed* form — a dense table of figures — rather than a picture of a sky. Keeps the load-bearing idea, discards the gradient. |
| 3 | **Duplicator / riso sports ephemera** | print process, spot ink | Club newsletters, race numbers, mimeographed training schedules. The one tradition where a huge cheap numeral is the hero at poster scale, on warm mid-tone stock. Warmth without a single badge. |
| 4 | **Enamel signage and graduated glass** | ceramic, glass, metal | Vitreous enamel plate, painted numerals, an etched cylinder scale. The physical object behind "a good instrument", and a graduated scale is a rep count readable across a room. |
| 5 | **The appellation / parcel survey map** | cartography | Hatched land, contour-shaded parcels, a legend keyed to tiers. The year grid and History become *terrain* — which is exactly the "lead with shape and texture, not figures" demotion §2.3 asks for. |
| 6 | **The darkroom timer face** | instrument dial | Luminous painted numerals in a safelight world, a dial that moves once per second. Owns the rest countdown and the "last five seconds perceptible without looking". |
| 7 | **The sekki agricultural almanac** | printed calendrical | The 24 micro-seasons name where you are in a cycle with a word and a colour instead of a number. A replacement for the dawn ramp that is *named* progress, and it belongs to a tea person. |

## 3. The seven as complete directions

Each joins a reusable visual world to a concrete Set-screen experience. Every
one of them is mid-tone by construction, because every one is built on a real
material that is neither black nor white.

### 1. Cellar Book

**World.** Aged ledger stock, a warm mid grey-buff ground. Printed rules in
oxblood and slate. Two inks and no more. Numerals set large in the ledger
tradition — lining figures, tabular, sitting *on* a rule rather than floating.
Column headers in small caps. The whole app is one book: every surface is a
spread, every session an entry.

**Set screen.** The set is a ruled line in a book that is being written as you
work. Last time's number is already printed on the line, in the lighter ink,
because it is a past entry. Today's number is being *entered* beside it in the
dark ink. The crossing is the moment your entry passes the printed one — and
the rule under it is what changes, not a colour wash.

**Progress without text.** The spread fills. Entries accumulate down the page as
the session runs; you can see from across the room how much of the page is left.

**Risk.** Ledger paper is the single most obvious answer for "a logging app",
and warm-cream-plus-serif is the exact rendition prior the calibration warns
about. It has to be built from bookcloth and printer's ink, not from parchment.

### 2. Almanac

**World.** The printed twilight table. Dense figure-setting on a cool mid stone
ground, hairline rules, a single warm signal ink. Type is the whole design:
tabular numerals, small caps, superior figures, rules that group rather than
decorate. Nothing is illustrated; everything is *set*.

**Set screen.** The set is a row in the day's table. The rep count is the figure
in the live column; last time's is the figure in the column beside it, printed
and fixed. The session's position in the dawn is a real almanac column — the
phase is *named* ("civil twilight"), not painted.

**Progress without text.** The table's live row advances down a printed page of
the session's rows — all of them visible, the way an almanac shows the whole day.

**Risk.** Denser than a half-awake person needs. The "eleven rows visible at
once" instinct collides head-on with one-screen-one-action, and it must not
become a scrolling list of the workout.

### 3. Duplicator

**World.** Spot ink on warm newsprint stock. Two or three flat inks, deliberate
misregistration, halftone dot where a field needs weight. Numerals at race-bib
scale in a heavy grotesque. No gradients exist in this world — it cannot produce
one. Mid-tone is native: newsprint is never white.

**Set screen.** The rep count is a race number, printed enormous, filling the
middle band of the screen. Last time's number is the same figure in the second
ink, offset — literally the previous printing, showing through. The crossing is
registration: the two figures snap into alignment and the second ink drops away.

**Progress without text.** Each finished set adds a printed mark to a bib-style
tally block; the sheet visibly fills with ink.

**Risk.** The loudest of the seven and the furthest from "chill vibes". Riso
warmth can tip into a poster that is exhausting at 6:10am, and misregistration
is a legibility cost paid on the one screen that must read at 1.5m.

### 4. Enamel

**World.** Vitreous enamel and graduated glass. A soft sage or slate porcelain
ground with the faint depth of a fired surface, numerals painted on rather than
composited in, a chipped-edge honesty at the boundaries. Controls are physical:
a pressed enamel button, an etched scale, a machined edge catching light.

**Set screen.** The rep count is painted on an enamel plate. Last time's number
is an etched graduation on the scale beside it — a physical mark on the
instrument, permanently there. The crossing is passing the graduation: the fill
rises past an etched line, which is a thing you can see with no colour change
at all.

**Progress without text.** The session is a graduated cylinder that fills. Not a
bar — a scale with real marks, read the way you read a measuring jug.

**Risk.** Skeuomorphism is one bad decision away, and iOS 26 is a poor host for
heavy material realism. It also risks answering "a good instrument" too
literally — the brief's own words as a picture.

### 5. Parcel Map

**World.** Survey cartography. Hatched and stippled land on a mid sand ground,
a restricted earth palette keyed to a legend, thin registration marks, place
names set in the map tradition. Fields have texture rather than fill.

**Set screen.** The session is a parcel walked end to end. The current exercise
is the parcel you are standing in, drawn at scale; the rest of the session is
the surrounding land, visible and unread. Last time's number is a boundary line
across the parcel — you are inside it or past it.

**Progress without text.** Position on the map *is* the progress. Nothing needs
to fill.

**Risk.** The strongest answer for History and the year grid and the weakest for
the Set screen — a map wants to be read, and a set does not. Likely to win a
surface it was not asked about.

### 6. Darkroom

**World.** Safelight. A deep warm amber-grey mid ground — dim but emphatically
not black, and the one candidate where "not dark" needs defending. Luminous
painted numerals, a dial face, glass and bakelite. Everything is legible under
one coloured light because that is the world's whole premise.

**Set screen.** The face of a process timer. The rep count is the dial's figure;
last time's number is the mark on the bezel you set before you start — the way
you set a timer's bezel to the time you mean to beat.

**Progress without text.** The bezel travels. One revolution is the session.

**Risk.** It is a *dark* world wearing a warm coat, and §2.1 says mid-tone, not
night. Amber-on-amber is a contrast problem before it is a design.

### 7. Sekki

**World.** The 24 micro-seasons as a printed calendrical system. Each phase has
a name, a colour and a mark. A pale mineral ground, ink in a narrow seasonal
range, a repeating woodblock mark per phase. Restraint is structural: the system
only ever shows you one season at a time.

**Set screen.** The session is a passage through named phases. Where the dawn
ramp said "you are 60% through" in colour, this says it with a *name* and a
mark — a thing you can read at 1.5m and also say out loud.

**Progress without text.** The mark changes. Discrete, not continuous — you can
tell two phases apart at a glance where you cannot tell 58% from 64%.

**Risk.** Borrowed from a culture that is his interest rather than his
inheritance, and the closest of the seven to decoration. Twenty-four phases is
also more resolution than a twenty-minute session can carry.
